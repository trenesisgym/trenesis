Recovery and sleep: where your gains are actually made

Why sleep and recovery decide how fast you progress, a simple sleep checklist, and what to do on rest days.

2 min read · Updated October 2026

Training breaks your muscles down a little; recovery is when they come back stronger. If you train hard but sleep five hours a night, you're leaving most of your progress on the table.

Sleep: your most underrated tool

During deep sleep your body releases growth hormone, repairs muscle and lets your nervous system recover from training. Regularly sleeping less than about 6 hours is linked to slower recovery, more injuries, lower testosterone and less motivation to train.

Sleep checklist

  • Aim for 7–9 hours, and prioritise sleep after heavy training days.
  • Keep a consistent bedtime and wake-up time, including weekends where you can.
  • Put screens away 30–60 minutes before bed; they make it harder to fall asleep.
  • Keep your bedroom cool. Around 17–19 °C works well for most people.
  • If you struggle to fall asleep, avoid training within 2 hours of bedtime.
  • Go easy on alcohol: even small amounts make sleep lighter and more broken.

Active recovery

Rest days don't have to mean lying on the sofa. Light movement such as walking, easy cycling or swimming gets blood flowing and helps you feel fresher. Aim for 20–40 minutes of easy activity, and skip anything that leaves you sore or tired.

Stress counts too

Exams, work deadlines and money worries all draw from the same recovery budget as training. Chronically stressed people tend to recover more slowly and stall sooner. In stressful weeks it's fine to train a little less hard; keep showing up and get back to full effort when things calm down.

Key insight

If you're stuck, check your sleep before you change your program. Most plateaus are lifestyle problems, not program problems.

Sources

  • Dáttilo M et al. Sleep and muscle recovery: endocrinological and molecular basis for a new and promising hypothesis. Medical Hypotheses, 2011.
