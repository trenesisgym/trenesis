Calories and TDEE: how to find out how much you should eat

Energy balance in plain language, how to estimate your daily calorie needs (TDEE) for your sex, age, height and activity, and why you don't need a big bulk to build muscle.

By Volodymyr and Linus3 min readUpdated November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Your weight goes up, down or stays the same depending on calories in versus calories out.
  • Estimate your maintenance from your sex, age, height, weight and activity, then check it against your scale for 2–3 weeks.
  • To build muscle eat about 5–10% above maintenance; to lose fat, about 10–20% below.

Whether your weight goes up, down or stays the same comes down to one relationship: the energy you eat versus the energy you burn. Food quality, hormones and micronutrients matter too, but for body composition this is the main lever.

Energy balance

A calorie is just a unit of energy. Everything you eat provides some; everything you do (breathing, digesting, moving, training) uses some.

State Calories eaten vs. burned Result
Deficit Less than you burn Weight loss
Maintenance About the same Weight stays stable
Surplus More than you burn Weight gain

Estimate your TDEE

TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is the total number of calories you burn in a day, including exercise. It has two parts: what your body burns at rest, and what you add by moving.

1. Your resting burn. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the most accurate everyday formula for healthy adults:

  • Women: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161
  • Men: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5

2. Multiply by your activity:

Activity level Multiply by
Mostly sitting, little exercise 1.2
Lightly active (1–2 workouts a week, or on your feet a lot) 1.375
Moderately active (3–4 workouts a week) 1.55
Very active (5+ workouts a week, or a physical job) 1.725

Example: a 25-year-old woman, 165 cm and 60 kg, who trains 3–4 times a week burns about 1,345 kcal at rest × 1.55 ≈ 2,090 kcal a day. Our calorie and protein calculator does this maths for you, in kg or lb, and also works if you'd rather not give your sex.

No calculator at hand? Bodyweight (kg) × 30–35 (or lb × 14–16) gives a rough figure for an active person, but it tends to be too high for women, older and shorter people.

These numbers are estimates. The best way to fine-tune them is to track what you eat and your bodyweight for 2–3 weeks: if your average weight is stable, you've found your maintenance.

Key insight

A deficit or surplus of about 500 kcal a day changes your weight by roughly 0.5 kg (about 1 lb) a week. Start conservatively: aggressive cuts cost muscle, aggressive surpluses mostly add fat.

Do you need to bulk?

No big bulk needed. Eating far above maintenance doesn't build muscle meaningfully faster; it mostly adds fat.

  • Beginners and people returning after a break can often build muscle at maintenance, sometimes even in a small deficit.
  • Most other lifters do well with a small surplus of about 5–10% (200–300 kcal a day for most people).

The takeaway

Muscle gain: eat about 5–10% above maintenance. Fat loss: eat about 10–20% below. Avoid extremes either way.
