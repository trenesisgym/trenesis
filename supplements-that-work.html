Supplements that actually work for lifting: protein powder and creatine

Why most supplements aren't worth your money, and the two with solid evidence for people who lift: protein powder and creatine monohydrate, including dose and timing.

2 min readUpdated October 2026

The supplement industry is built mostly on marketing. The vast majority of products on the shelf aren't necessary. Supplements are a small, optional extra: they won't make a real difference if your training, eating, sleep and consistency aren't already in place.

For people who strength train, two supplements have strong, consistent evidence behind them.

Protein powder

Protein powder isn't a special muscle-building substance; it's just a convenient way to get protein in. Whey (from milk) is the most common, but plant-based powders such as pea and rice blends work just as well.

  • Mix it with water or milk, or add it to oats, yoghurt or baking.
  • A typical scoop gives about 20–30 g of protein for roughly 100–130 kcal.
  • It's a convenience, not a replacement for whole foods.

Creatine monohydrate

Creatine is the most-studied supplement in sports science and one of the very few with clear performance benefits. It tops up your muscles' phosphocreatine stores, which helps you produce energy in short, hard efforts: exactly what lifting demands.

  • Dose: 3–5 g a day. No loading phase needed.
  • Timing: whenever suits you; it doesn't change the result.
  • Expect 1–2 kg of extra bodyweight in the first weeks. That's water stored in your muscles, not fat.
  • Benefits: a few more reps at a given weight, better recovery between sets and, over time, more strength and muscle.
  • Buy plain creatine monohydrate. It's the cheapest and the best-researched form; ignore pricey variants.

Key insight

Supplements sit at the bottom of the hierarchy. No supplement makes up for a poor diet: fix your calories and protein first.

Check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking supplements if you have a medical condition or take medication.
